Road safety priorities

Where should the next safety dollar go?

Boston, Cambridge and Somerville record about 7,600 crashes a year between them. A safety budget can treat a handful of locations annually. This tool ranks every intersection by how much harm happens there per vehicle passing through — and shows why the three cities need different fixes.

Two rankings that disagree

Total harm is what MassDOT publishes — it finds where the most damage accumulates, which tends to be the busiest roads. Harm per million vehicles divides that by traffic volume — it finds places that are dangerous rather than merely busy. Switch between them and watch the map change.

02 · Compare cities

Three cities, three different problems

Comparing how many crashes each city has would mostly compare how big they are — and how completely each police department reports. Comparing the composition of crashes avoids both problems: a share is unaffected by how many reports reach the state.

    03 · Verification

    Checking this against MassDOT's own list

    A ranking is only worth acting on if it can be reproduced. MassDOT publishes its own crash-cluster layers computed with a documented method. Two checks were run against them.

    The weighting formula reproduces exactly

    MassDOT publishes an EPDO value on every cluster polygon alongside its component counts. Recomputing (K_A + B_C) × 21 + O × 1 reproduced the published figure on 133 of 133 clusters in the three cities — so the weighting used here is MassDOT's, not an invention.

    The locations line up

    Clustering crashes independently at 25 m placed a location within 75 m of 125 of those 133 official clusters. The eight misses are pedestrian and bicycle clusters, which MassDOT builds with a 100 m radius over a ten-year window — a deliberately wider net than this analysis uses.

    So why build this at all?

    MassDOT's Top 200 is a statewide list, and only 11 of its 206 clusters fall inside these three cities — it is dominated by high-volume suburban arterials. A city engineer with a local budget needs the ranking computed within the city, normalised by local traffic. That is what the Explore tab does.

    04 · Data & method

    One-page data and methodology note

    The question

    A municipal safety engineer has a fixed budget and several thousand intersections. Crash counts alone rank the busiest roads, which the engineer already knows. The question is: which intersections carry the most harm relative to how much traffic passes through them, and does the answer differ between the three cities?

    Source

    MassDOT IMPACT open crash data — police crash reports filed with the Massachusetts Registry of Motor Vehicles. One ArcGIS FeatureServer per calendar year, public domain, no key required. Field definitions come from the Massachusetts Law Enforcement Crash Report E-Manual (UMassSafe / EOPSS / MassDOT, written to MMUCC 5th edition). Traffic volumes are joined onto each crash by MassDOT from its Road Inventory.

    Collection

    fetch_crashes.py queried each year's service filtered server-side to CITY_TOWN_NAME IN ('BOSTON','CAMBRIDGE','SOMERVILLE'). Two implementation notes, because both silently truncate data:

    1. The 2023 service is named MASSDOT_ODP_OPEN_2023v — with a trailing v. The predictable name 404s.
    2. The 2021 service caps a response at 1,000 records while the others cap at 2,000. A first attempt that assumed a short page meant the end of the data silently kept 1,000 of 6,169 rows for that year. The final script requests the full OBJECTID list first, fetches in ID batches, and verifies each year against the server's own returnCountOnly total.

    Cleaning — and what was discarded

    1. Padding. Every text field arrives space-padded to a fixed width; all 57 were stripped.
    2. Coordinates. 35,389 of 37,929 crashes (93.3%) have usable coordinates. The remaining 2,540 (6.7%) stay in the citywide totals and are excluded from the map and the ranking.
    3. Speed limits. The raw column ranges from −125 to 265 mph. Values outside 5–75 were discarded — 62 records.
    4. Injury severity. The severity column uses two different label schemes across the five years; both were mapped onto KABCO.
    5. Vulnerable road users. Parsed from NON_MTRST_TYPE_CL, which lists each non-motorist as VU2: Pedestrian, VU3: Bicyclist, joined with /.
    6. Contributing circumstances. DRVR_CNTRB_CIRC_CL is formatted D1: (reason) / D2: (reason), one block per vehicle. 25 distinct tokens extracted. Recorded on 29,527 crashes (77.8%), so every factor percentage is taken over that base.

    The AADT placeholder — the decision that mattered most

    MassDOT carries an AADT on 33,681 crashes (88.8%). But 4,822 of them — 12.7% of all crashes — carry the identical value 1,154, always with an empty AADT_DERIV, almost always on a road classed Local (4,822 of 5,198 Local-road crashes). It is a statewide placeholder for streets with no traffic count, not a measurement. Dividing by it made quiet residential side streets the most dangerous places in the region — they occupied most of the top of the first risk table produced.

    The rule adopted: an AADT counts as exposure only when AADT_DERIV records how it was obtained. That leaves 28,647 crashes (75.5%) with usable exposure, in four provenance tiers:

    Grouping crashes into intersections

    Crashes were restricted to those coded as a four-way, T, Y or five-point-or-more junction (12,406 of 37,929), then grouped by single-link clustering at a 25 metre radius with a KD-tree and union-find. Both the radius and the junction filter match MassDOT's published method, so the output is comparable to theirs. Result: 3,758 locations, of which 2,828 (75.3%) have a usable exposure denominator.

    Each cluster takes the highest usable AADT among its crashes, not the median: a crash recorded on the minor leg carries that leg's low count, and the median would understate how much traffic the junction handles.

    A wide junction — a divided boulevard, a highway interchange — is larger than 25 m across, so it splits into more than one cluster and every piece carries the same pair of street names. Rather than an arbitrary number, each piece is labelled by where it sits relative to the others: (E approach), (NW approach). 361 locations are labelled this way; a further 126, where the compass bearing does not separate them, fall back to (site 1), (site 2). Merging them would have produced nicer names at the cost of no longer matching MassDOT's 25 m method, which is what makes the verification below possible.

    The score

    EPDO = (injury crashes × 21) + (property-damage-only crashes × 1)
    exposure = AADTmajor × 365 × 5 years ÷ 1,000,000
    risk = EPDO ÷ exposure  — EPDO points per million vehicles

    The 21:1 weighting is MassDOT's own, taken unchanged rather than invented. Dividing by exposure is this analysis's addition — MassDOT ranks by raw EPDO. Crashes whose severity was not reported (3,925, 10.3%) are given a weight of 1: the conservative choice, since an unrecorded severity cannot inflate a location's score.

    Priority bands on the map are fixed absolute thresholds, not percentiles, so filtering never repaints a location: critical is EPDO ≥ 150 (89 locations, 2.4%) or ≥ 6 per million vehicles; elevated is 60–149 (321, 8.5%) or 2.5–6; everything else is lower.

    Key definitions

    EPDO — Equivalent Property Damage Only
    MassDOT's severity weighting. Any crash with an injury or fatality counts 21; property-damage-only counts 1. Unit: points.
    AADT — Annual Average Daily Traffic
    Vehicles per day on the road segment, from the MassDOT Road Inventory. Unit: vehicles/day. Each intersection uses the highest usable AADT among its crashes — the major approach.
    Harm per million vehicles
    EPDO ÷ (AADT × 365 × 5 years ÷ 1,000,000). Unit: EPDO points per million vehicles.
    Intersection cluster
    Crashes within 25 m of one another at four-way, T, Y or five-point junctions. Radius and junction filter both match MassDOT.
    KABCO
    The national injury scale: K fatal, A suspected serious, B suspected minor, C possible, O no apparent injury.
    Walking & cycling involved
    The record lists at least one pedestrian, cyclist, scooter rider or skateboarder. Unit: share of crashes.

    05 · Reflection

    What the data supports — and what it cannot prove

    What it supports

    • A shortlist. The ranking is a defensible, reproducible order in which to send engineers to look at intersections. It reproduces MassDOT's own arithmetic exactly and lands on their own locations, so it is not an idiosyncratic result.
    • That the three cities differ in kind, not just in size. Composition shares are robust to how completely a police department reports: if Boston files only 70% of its crashes, the share involving a cyclist is still roughly right. The gap between Cambridge's 7.6% cyclist involvement and Boston's 1.7% is far too large to be a reporting artefact.
    • That a volume-driven list misses things. Only 11 of MassDOT's 206 statewide Top-200 clusters fall inside these three cities. That is a fact about the statewide list, not an inference.

    What it cannot prove

    • That any listed intersection is actually unsafe. A high score is a reason to send an engineer, not a diagnosis. The data says nothing about sight lines, signal timing, curb geometry or how people actually behave there.
    • That a treatment would work. Nothing here is a before-and-after study. The recommendations pair a location with a plausible countermeasure; whether that countermeasure reduces harm is a separate question this dataset cannot answer.
    • That Cambridge is more dangerous for cyclists than Boston. Cambridge has a much higher share of crashes involving cyclists — but it also has far more cycling. Without bicycle exposure data (trip counts, not vehicle AADT), a share of crashes cannot become a risk per cyclist. It is equally consistent with Cambridge being safer per trip and simply having many more trips. This is the single biggest thing the site cannot settle.
    • That speeding causes Boston's crashes. “Speeding” is an officer's after-the-fact judgement recorded on 78% of reports, not a measurement. Three police departments may apply the label differently, and that alone could produce the gap between Boston's 9.0% and Cambridge's 2.1%.
    • Anything about the tail of the ranking. A location with six crashes moves many places if one injury had been coded one level differently. The top band is stable; below it the ordering is noise.
    • Anything about crashes nobody reported. Unreported collisions skew heavily toward minor damage and toward cyclists who were not seriously hurt — exactly the population one of the findings is about.

    What I would do with more time

    Get bicycle and pedestrian exposure. Cambridge publishes 238,799 rows of citywide bicycle counts with coordinates and Somerville publishes an annual bike/pedestrian count; Boston publishes hourly bike counts but no citywide pedestrian count. Joining those to the crash locations would convert “share of crashes involving a cyclist” into “crashes per thousand cyclists” — the number a city actually needs, and the one this version cannot produce.

    Second, cross-validate against the cities' own crash files. Somerville's mtik-28va is 100% geocoded and carries full KABCO counts and a crash ID; Cambridge's gb5w-yva3 is only 51.5% geocoded and has no ID at all. Comparing them against MassDOT would quantify how much each city under-reports upward, turning the reporting caveat from a qualitative warning into a correction factor.
